A medium effect is nineteen points over the median

Supplement marketing sells a word. The study underneath carries a d, and a d converts to a percentile, the only form of the number a purchase decision can use. Written down, the conversion prices every label in the cabinet against the same ruler.

The conversion is one line: the share of untreated people an average user outranks. A d of 0.2 lands at the 58th percentile, 0.5 at the 69th, 0.8 at the 79th. Push the effect to 1.0 and the number reaches 84. The steps are worth 8 points, then 11, then 10, then 5. The first increment does the most work and the last one does the least, which is the reverse of how the word on the label escalates.

Stack budgets price cleanly on the same ruler. A $10 jar tied to a d of 0.8 buys 29 points, about 35 cents per point each month. A $40 bottle tied to a d of 0.2 buys 8 points, five dollars a point. Same cabinet, 14.5 times apart on the denominator that matters. The expensive capsule is where the stack bleeds, and the arithmetic never gets written down.

Body feel cannot see an eight-point shift. A month on a d of 0.2 and a month off it differ by less than sleep debt, travel, or a bad week, so self-report is the wrong instrument and the dose log is the right one. The protocols that survive audit name a dose and a measurable: four sessions above 175°F, grip numbers, gait speed. Anything asking for cabinet space without both gets the percentile treatment before it earns a permanent slot.

HackedSelf runs its reviews through this conversion, effect-size rankings beside minimum doses and the measurable fields that catch a placebo month. Read the number before the word, price the point, and cut what costs five dollars for eight.
